Aojiru: the Japanese green drink made from young barley

Published 26 October 2026 · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Aojiru is a “green juice” that many people in Japan drink every morning as routinely as we drink coffee. What it is made from, what it contains, how to mix it and who should be careful.

Aojiru (青汁, “green juice”) is a Japanese drink made from powdered young leaves, most often barley grass, kale or ashitaba, mixed with water or milk. In Japan it is drunk as an easy way to add a little greenery, fibre and plant compounds to the day when the diet is short of vegetables. Aojiru does not replace vegetables or “cleanse” the body, but it can be a convenient daily habit.

What aojiru is

The word combines two Japanese words: “ao”, meaning blue-green, and “jiru”, meaning juice. The drink appeared in Japan in the mid‑20th century as a way to supplement a diet poor in vegetables, and later became a mass-market product: it is sold in single-serve sticks in supermarkets and pharmacies, advertised on television and drunk by older and younger people alike.

Modern aojiru is not freshly pressed juice but a powder: young leaves are dried and ground, sometimes with flavourings added. One stick usually makes one glass.

For many Japanese people, aojiru is part of the morning ritual, like tea or coffee for us. It is drunk with breakfast or added to yoghurt, smoothies or soya milk. For building small healthy habits into your morning, see Energy without too much coffee: 7 daily habits.

Barley grass, kale, matcha: the difference

Aojiru is made from different plants, and the taste and composition depend on the base.

BaseFeatures
Young barley grass (大麦若葉)The mildest taste and today's most popular base; leaves are harvested before the ear forms
KaleThe classic base of the first aojiru; a grassier, slightly bitter taste
AshitabaA Japanese plant from the carrot family; a distinctive taste
MatchaNot a base but a flavouring; it makes the taste softer and more tea-like

Do not confuse aojiru with matcha. Matcha is powdered green tea and contains caffeine. Barley grass is a cereal and contains no caffeine. When an aojiru pack says “matcha flavour”, matcha is only a flavouring.

Aojiru also differs from chlorella and spirulina, which are microalgae with a different composition and much more protein. For a comparison, see Chlorella or spirulina: what is the difference and Spirulina: composition, benefits and how to take it.

What it contains

Young barley grass powder naturally contains:

  • dietary fibre — aojiru's main practical value;
  • chlorophyll — the green plant pigment;
  • vitamins — beta-carotene, B vitamins, C, E and K;
  • minerals — potassium, calcium, magnesium and iron;
  • polyphenols — plant compounds with antioxidant activity in the lab.

Realistic expectations: a 3 g stick is a small serving. It adds a little fibre and plant compounds but does not replace the 400 g of vegetables and fruit a day that WHO recommends. Aojiru complements a plate of vegetables rather than replacing it.

Two components matter for safety:

  • vitamin K — involved in blood clotting. People taking warfarin need a stable vitamin K intake; in Japan, such patients are often advised to discuss aojiru with their doctor;
  • potassium — with kidney disease and a potassium-restricted diet, green drinks should also be agreed with a doctor.

How to mix it and what to drink it with

The basic method: empty a stick into a glass, add 75–⁠100 ml of cold water or milk and stir well or shake in a shaker. The powder dissolves more easily if you first mix it with a little liquid and then add the rest.

A few rules:

  • do not use hot liquid — the manufacturer recommends cold or warm water; do not pour boiling water over the powder;
  • drink it straight away — the powder settles;
  • one stick a day — do not exceed the recommended amount;
  • have it with food — breakfast is a convenient time.

Ways to make aojiru taste milder:

  • with dairy, oat or soya milk — the taste becomes similar to a matcha latte;
  • with kefir or drinking yoghurt;
  • in a smoothie with banana, apple or kiwi;
  • with apple or orange juice half and half with water;
  • with a spoonful of honey (for adults) if the taste seems too grassy.

The taste: how to get used to it

Your first glass of aojiru may taste unusual: fresh grass with a slight bitterness. Matcha-flavoured versions are milder, and with milk the drink resembles green tea with milk.

How to get used to it:

  • start with half a stick in a glass of milk or juice;
  • chill it — a cold drink tastes fresher;
  • shake it — the texture is nicer without lumps;
  • give yourself a week — people get used to a grassy taste the same way they get used to dark chocolate or unsweetened coffee.

The fibre in aojiru may cause mild bloating in the first few days. Drink more water, and the gut usually adapts within a few days. How to add fibre gradually is explained in Fibre and digestion: Japanese habits.

FAQ

How do I drink aojiru?

Dissolve one stick (3 g) in 75–⁠100 ml of cold water, milk or juice, stir well and drink straight away. Breakfast is a convenient time; do not use hot liquid.

Can I drink aojiru every day?

Yes, aojiru is designed for daily use, one stick a day. If you take anticoagulants or have kidney disease, ask your doctor first.

Does matcha-flavoured aojiru contain caffeine?

Barley grass contains no caffeine. The Kin no Aojiru in our catalogue is 100% barley grass and caffeine-free; the matcha-like taste comes naturally from the young leaves. If another brand's aojiru contains real matcha, it may have a little caffeine, so check the ingredients.

Can children drink aojiru?

The products in our catalogue are intended for adults. Children are better off with vegetables and fruit; ask a paediatrician about supplements.

How is aojiru different from chlorella?

Aojiru is powder from the leaves of land plants (barley, kale) and a source of fibre. Chlorella is a microalga with a high protein content and a different composition.
