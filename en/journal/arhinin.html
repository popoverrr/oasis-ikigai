L-arginine: why people take it

Published 22 October 2026 · Updated 22 October 2026 · 8 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

L-arginine is taken by athletes, men over 40 and anyone who has heard about “nitric oxide”. What is known about this amino acid, what to expect from a supplement and which medicines it must not be combined with.

L-arginine is an amino acid the body uses to make nitric oxide, which widens blood vessels, so people take it to support circulation, endurance and men's health. Research shows only modest and mixed effects, while the risks are real: arginine can add to the effect of blood pressure medicines and nitrates. So with heart and blood vessel conditions, or if you take blood pressure medicines, nitrates or erectile dysfunction drugs, take arginine only after talking to your doctor.

What L-arginine is

L-arginine is one of the 20 amino acids that make up proteins. It is called conditionally essential: a healthy adult makes enough arginine, but during growth, after injuries or burns, and in some illnesses the need can exceed the body's own production.

Arginine is involved in several important processes:

  • making nitric oxide (NO) — a signalling molecule that relaxes blood vessel walls;
  • the urea cycle — removing nitrogen produced when proteins are broken down;
  • making creatine — which muscles use as a quick energy source;
  • wound healing and immune cell function.

Where we get arginine from food: meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, pulses (especially soya, chickpeas and lentils), nuts and seeds — peanuts, walnuts and pumpkin seeds. On a normal varied diet with enough protein, arginine deficiency in healthy adults is unlikely.

Nitric oxide and blood flow

The endothelium — the inner lining of blood vessels — makes nitric oxide from arginine. NO relaxes the smooth muscle in vessel walls, the vessels widen and blood flow improves. This mechanism matters for blood pressure, heart function, muscles under load and erections.

Hence the logic behind supplements: more arginine, more nitric oxide, better blood flow. In reality it is more complicated:

  • much of the arginine in a supplement is broken down in the gut and liver before it reaches the blood vessels;
  • in healthy people, the enzymes that make NO usually have enough arginine already, so extra arginine does not necessarily increase NO production;
  • the effect may be more noticeable in people with impaired endothelial function, but they are also more likely to have heart disease and take medicines that interact with arginine.

What the research says

  • Blood pressure. A meta-analysis of randomised trials (Am Heart J, 2011) found that arginine supplements lower blood pressure slightly. This is not a substitute for treating hypertension, but it is a reason for caution: combined with blood pressure medicines, the effects add up.
  • After a heart attack. In the VINTAGE MI trial (JAMA, 2006), people who had had a heart attack were given arginine or a placebo. Arginine did not improve vascular measures, and there were more deaths in the arginine group, so the trial was stopped early. The authors concluded that arginine should not be recommended after a heart attack.
  • Sport. A 2020 systematic review (Nutrients) found some benefit of arginine for aerobic and anaerobic performance with specific dosing schedules, but the studies were small and varied. More on supplements for training in Vitamins and minerals for people who exercise.
  • Men's health. Arginine is a common ingredient in men's formulas. Studies in erectile dysfunction are small and the results conflicting; erection problems can be an early sign of heart disease, so start with a doctor rather than a supplement. On men's basic needs, see Vitamins for men: what matters.

The honest summary: L-arginine is neither an energy drink nor a medicine. It is an amino acid we get from food, and a supplement can be an add-on for active adults without contraindications.

Who should be careful

Do not take L-arginine without a doctor if you:

  • have had a heart attack or have coronary heart disease;
  • take blood pressure medicines — arginine can add to the drop in pressure;
  • take nitrates (nitroglycerin, isosorbide) — risk of a sharp fall in blood pressure;
  • take erectile dysfunction drugs (sildenafil, tadalafil) — the same risk;
  • have low blood pressure;
  • get frequent herpes outbreaks — in the lab, the herpes virus uses arginine to replicate; clinical data are limited, but with frequent outbreaks it is better to ask your doctor;
  • have diabetes and take blood-sugar-lowering medicines;
  • have kidney or liver disease;
  • are pregnant or breastfeeding;
  • have surgery planned.

Possible side effects include bloating, stomach discomfort, nausea and diarrhoea. They usually depend on the dose; splitting the daily amount into several doses with food reduces them.

How to take it

Directions for ORIHIRO L-Arginine 1000 + Zinc on the pack: 4 capsules a day with water. Do not exceed the recommended daily dose. 120 capsules last about 30 days; the recommended course is 3 months.

Practical tips:

  • split the 4 capsules into 2 doses — for example 2 with breakfast and 2 with lunch;
  • take with food if you have a sensitive stomach; zinc on an empty stomach makes some people nauseous;
  • on training days, many people take part of the daily amount 1–⁠2 hours before exercise, but do not exceed the total dose;
  • do not combine with other zinc supplements or other arginine formulas;
  • keep it a few hours apart from iron and calcium — zinc competes with them for absorption.

Arginine is not a stimulant, so do not expect a coffee-like effect. For staying alert through the day, see Energy without extra coffee: 7 daily habits. Other energy products are in the Energy section and on the ORIHIRO brand page.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines. L-arginine can increase the effect of blood pressure medicines, nitrates and erectile dysfunction drugs, and it is not recommended after a heart attack. With heart or blood vessel disease, low blood pressure, diabetes, kidney or liver disease, frequent herpes outbreaks, or during pregnancy and breastfeeding, take it only after talking to your doctor.

FAQ

When is the best time to take arginine?

Follow the directions on the pack: for ORIHIRO, 4 capsules a day, conveniently in 2 doses with meals. On training days, part of the daily amount can be taken 1–⁠2 hours before exercise without exceeding the daily dose.

Can women take arginine?

Yes, arginine is an amino acid needed by both women and men. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, or if you take medicines, only after talking to your doctor.

Can I take arginine if I have blood pressure problems?

Arginine can lower blood pressure slightly and add to the effect of hypertension medicines. With high or low blood pressure, or if you take any blood pressure medicine, only on your doctor's advice.

Can I take arginine with erectile dysfunction drugs?

Not without a doctor: together with sildenafil, tadalafil or nitrates, arginine can cause a sharp fall in blood pressure.

Is arginine found in food?

Yes, in meat, fish, eggs, dairy, pulses, nuts and seeds. On a varied diet, healthy adults get enough.
