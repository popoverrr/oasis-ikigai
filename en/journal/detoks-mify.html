Detox: what is true and what is marketing

Published 26 October 2026 · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Juices, “cleanses”, slimming teas — the detox industry promises a clean slate in a week. How the body handles this on its own, what genuinely helps, and how to think honestly about “detox” supplements.

A body detox in the sense of “flushing out toxins in a week” is a marketing term: the liver, kidneys, gut, lungs and skin neutralise and remove unwanted substances every day, and they do not need special juices or “cleanses” to do it. The US centre NCCIH notes that there is no compelling evidence that detox programmes work, and some can cause harm. What helps these organs most is ordinary: water, fibre, sleep, movement and less alcohol.

How the body cleans itself

Several systems constantly get rid of what the body does not need:

  • the liver — the main “laboratory”: it converts medicines, alcohol, metabolic by-products and substances from food into forms that are easier to remove;
  • the kidneys — filter the blood every day and remove waste products in urine;
  • the gut — removes what has not been absorbed, with fibre and gut bacteria keeping things regular;
  • the lungs — remove carbon dioxide and volatile substances;
  • the skin — a barrier; sweat is mostly water and salts.

These organs work all the time, without “cleansing days”. If they are diseased, a person needs treatment, not a detox tea.

In medicine, “detox” has a specific meaning: treating poisoning or alcohol or drug dependence in hospital under medical supervision. It has nothing to do with juices and jars.

Where the detox trend came from

The idea is simple and appealing: we get “clogged up” by food, city life and stress, and then “clean ourselves out” in a week. It sells well because it:

  • promises quick results — lightness, a few kilos lost, clear skin;
  • gives a sense of control — you bought a kit and did something for yourself;
  • builds on real feelings — after a week without alcohol, fast food and sweets, people really do feel better.

But the improvement comes not from a “cleanse” but from temporarily cutting out alcohol, sugar and snacks, drinking more water and eating vegetables. The weight lost is mostly water and gut contents, and it comes back quickly.

Some “detox programmes” are risky: prolonged fasting, laxative teas, enemas and “colon cleanses” can upset fluid and salt balance, and large amounts of certain juices can strain the kidneys.

What really helps the liver and gut

Habits that have real evidence behind them:

  • less alcohol — the most important step for the liver; WHO does not identify any safe level of drinking;
  • a healthy weight — overweight and obesity are linked to fatty liver disease;
  • physical activity — regular movement helps both the liver and the gut;
  • fewer ultra-processed foods, sugary drinks and added sugar;
  • care with medicines and supplements — do not exceed doses of painkillers (especially paracetamol), and do not combine many herbal supplements without a doctor;
  • hepatitis B vaccination — on your doctor's advice;
  • regular blood tests — a liver function test shows how your liver is doing better than any feeling.

Fibre, water, sleep

Three basics that do more than any “cleanse”:

Fibre. It adds bulk to gut contents, supports regular bowel movements and feeds beneficial bacteria. WHO recommends at least 25 g of fibre a day for adults from vegetables, fruit, pulses and whole grains. More in Fibre and digestion: Japanese habits.

Water. The kidneys work better when you drink enough; together with fibre, water helps prevent constipation. Go by thirst and pale urine.

Sleep. All body systems recover during sleep. Chronic sleep loss is linked to poorer metabolism and stronger sugar cravings.

A Japanese example: traditional cuisine has lots of vegetables, seaweed, pulses and fermented foods (miso, natto), small portions and the habit of “hara hachi bu” — eating until 80% full. No “detox days”, just a daily foundation.

"Detox" supplements: an honest look

Shelves labelled “detox” usually hold:

  • activated charcoal — a binder that holds substances in the gut. In medicine it is used for certain poisonings under medical supervision. There is no convincing evidence that taking charcoal regularly helps weight loss or “cleanses” the body. Important: charcoal also binds medicines and vitamins, so it must be taken separately from them. More in Charcoal in supplements: what to know;
  • chlorella — a microalga that provides plant protein, chlorophyll, iron and other nutrients. As part of the diet, yes; as a “cleanse”, there is little evidence. For a comparison with another microalga, see Chlorella or spirulina: what is the difference;
  • green drinks (aojiru) — a source of fibre and plant compounds; see Aojiru: the Japanese green drink made from young barley;
  • chitosan — a fibre from crustacean shells; more in Chitosan: how it works and what science says.

How to choose without the hype

A few questions to help you avoid marketing traps:

  • Does the product promise to “remove toxins”, “cleanse the liver” or effortless weight loss? That is a reason to be wary: such promises are not supported by evidence.
  • Is it clear exactly what is in it and how much? An honest manufacturer lists every ingredient.
  • Are there warnings and contraindications? Their absence is a bad sign, not a sign of safety.
  • Is it really a laxative in detox packaging? Senna, buckthorn and similar plants are not meant for daily use.
  • Does it interact with your medicines? Charcoal, chlorella and herbs are questions for your doctor or pharmacist.

Supplements can be part of a daily ritual if you understand why you are taking them and do not expect too much from them. A real “detox” is a diet with vegetables, water, sleep, movement and less alcohol.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines. Activated charcoal binds medicines and vitamins, so it must be taken separately from them. Chlorella contains vitamin K and may interact with warfarin. With liver, kidney or bowel disease, diabetes, or during pregnancy and breastfeeding, talk to your doctor before taking any supplements.

FAQ

Does the body need a detox?

Not in the sense of special “cleanses”: the liver, kidneys and gut do this work every day. You can support them with water, fibre, sleep, movement and less alcohol.

Does charcoal help with weight loss?

There is no convincing evidence. Charcoal binds substances in the gut but does not burn fat, and it also binds medicines and vitamins, so take it separately from them.

Is it true that chlorella "cleanses" the body?

There is little scientific evidence for “cleansing”. Chlorella provides plant protein, chlorophyll and minerals and can be part of a diet, but it does not replace the work of the liver and kidneys.

Are detox juices harmful?

One fresh juice is not harmful, but multi-day “juice diets” provide little protein and a lot of sugar, and large amounts of some juices can strain the kidneys. Whole vegetables and fruit are better.

How can I tell if my liver is healthy?

It is hard to tell by how you feel. A liver function blood test (ALT, AST, bilirubin and other markers) and a doctor's examination will show whether all is well.
