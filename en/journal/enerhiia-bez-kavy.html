Energy without too much coffee: 7 daily habits

Published 22 October 2026 · Updated 22 October 2026 · 8 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

A third coffee before lunch and still no energy? Here are 7 daily habits that lift energy more reliably than caffeine, plus what is known about Japanese energy supplements.

To boost your energy without extra coffee, start with a fixed wake-up time and morning light, a breakfast with protein, water through the day and short movement breaks. Coffee is not harmful: according to EFSA, up to 400 mg of caffeine a day is safe for healthy adults, but coffee after lunch disturbs sleep, and the next day you feel even more tired. If tiredness lasts for weeks, you need blood tests, not another cup.

Why we get tired

Tiredness is not one problem but a signal that can have many causes. The most common are everyday ones:

  • too little or irregular sleep — late weeknights and weekend lie-ins throw off the internal clock;
  • not moving enough — paradoxically, the less we move, the less energy we have;
  • irregular meals — a skipped breakfast followed by a sugary snack, after which energy crashes;
  • not drinking enough water;
  • long-term stress — tension without recovery is draining;
  • coffee instead of sleep — a vicious circle: tired → coffee → worse sleep → even more tired.

There are also medical causes: anaemia and iron deficiency, low vitamin B12 or D, thyroid problems, diabetes, sleep apnoea, depression and the after-effects of infections. They are covered at the end of the article.

Sleep and morning light

Habit 1. A fixed wake-up time. Your internal clock loves predictability. Getting up at the same time, even at weekends, makes it easier to fall asleep at night and to feel alert in the morning. Adults need 7–⁠9 hours of sleep. Ten rules for good sleep are in Sleep hygiene: 10 rules for better rest.

Habit 2. Light in the first hour. Daylight is the strongest “the day has started” signal. 15–⁠30 minutes outdoors, or at least by a window, in the morning helps you wake up and sets your clock so you feel sleepy on time in the evening. In winter, when it gets light late, go outside at lunchtime.

A short daytime nap (20 minutes, before 3 pm) can refresh you, but a longer afternoon nap disrupts night-time sleep.

Water and food

Habit 3. Water before coffee. Start the morning with a glass of water and keep a bottle on your desk. Even mild dehydration shows up as tiredness, headaches and poorer concentration. Tea, soup, fruit and vegetables add fluid too.

Habit 4. Breakfast and lunch with protein and fibre. Energy stays steady when your plate has protein (eggs, cottage cheese, fish, meat, pulses), complex carbohydrates (oats, buckwheat, wholegrain bread) and vegetables. A sweet pastry with coffee gives a quick lift and an equally quick crash. If there are long gaps between meals, plan a snack: yoghurt, nuts or fruit.

A few practical ideas:

  • porridge with nuts and berries instead of a sweet breakfast;
  • eggs with vegetables and wholegrain bread;
  • for lunch, half a plate of vegetables, a quarter protein and a quarter grains or potatoes;
  • a green aojiru drink or a glass of kefir as a snack — more on the Japanese drink in Aojiru: the Japanese green drink made from young barley.

Moving through the day

Habit 5. Movement breaks every hour. 3–⁠5 minutes of movement every hour — taking the stairs, a short walk, a few squats or stretches — restores alertness better than another cup of coffee. WHO recommends 150–⁠300 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults; a brisk 30-minute walk five days a week already meets it.

Regular exercise reduces fatigue over time, even if the first sessions seem to do the opposite. Start small: a walk after lunch, walking to work, an evening stretch. For which vitamins and minerals matter to people who train, see Vitamins and minerals for active people.

Coffee: how much and when

Habit 6. Coffee before lunch and within limits. There is no need to ban coffee — what matters is how much and when.

EFSA guidance for healthy adults:

  • up to 400 mg of caffeine a day raises no safety concerns;
  • up to 200 mg at a time for a single serving;
  • in pregnancy, up to 200 mg a day.

Roughly how much that is:

  • a cup of filter coffee (200 ml) — about 90 mg;
  • an espresso (60 ml) — about 80 mg;
  • a cup of black tea — about 50 mg;
  • an energy drink (250 ml) — about 80 mg.

A few tips:

  • do not have coffee the moment you wake up — have it after a glass of water and breakfast;
  • have your last coffee before 2 pm so caffeine does not disturb your sleep;
  • cut down gradually — stopping abruptly can cause headaches and drowsiness for a few days;
  • instead of a third cup — a walk, water, herbal tea or a glass of aojiru.

Habit 7. “Closing the day” in the evening. Tomorrow morning's energy depends on tonight: an hour without bright screens, a light dinner, a warm bath or shower. Sleep becomes deeper, and your morning coffee becomes a pleasure rather than a necessity.

Energy supplements: what is known

Supplements cannot replace sleep and movement, but they can be part of a daily self-care ritual. An honest look at popular ingredients:

  • ginseng — according to NCCIH, studies on fatigue and endurance are mixed; ginseng may lower blood sugar and interact with medicines;
  • maca — a traditional Andean plant; there are few good-quality studies;
  • cordyceps — a mushroom popular in East Asian traditions; data on its effect on human endurance are limited. More in Cordyceps: what is known about this mushroom;
  • L-arginine — an amino acid used to make nitric oxide; sports studies are small. More in L-arginine: why people take it;
  • B vitamins and zinc — contribute to normal energy metabolism, but only add energy if you were short of them.

When tiredness means seeing a doctor

See your GP if:

  • tiredness lasts more than 2–⁠4 weeks and rest does not help;
  • it comes with breathlessness, pale skin, dizziness or brittle nails — possible anaemia; see Iron deficiency in women: signs, tests and what to do;
  • you feel cold, gain weight, and your hair is dry and falling out — it is worth checking your thyroid;
  • you are constantly thirsty and pass urine often — check your blood sugar;
  • you snore and wake up exhausted — possible sleep apnoea;
  • tiredness comes with low mood and loss of interest.

Doctors usually order a full blood count, ferritin, glucose, TSH, and vitamins B12 and D. These simple tests often find a cause that coffee only masks.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines. Ginseng, cordyceps and L-arginine may interact with diabetes and blood pressure medicines, anticoagulants, antidepressants and immunosuppressants — if you take any medicines, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk to your doctor. Tiredness that lasts for weeks is a reason to get checked.

FAQ

What can I drink instead of coffee?

Water, herbal tea, green tea with less caffeine, kefir or a green aojiru drink. But the biggest energy boost comes not from drinks but from sleep, morning light and moving through the day.

Which vitamins help with tiredness?

Vitamins only help with tiredness if you were short of them — most often iron, vitamin B12 and vitamin D. So get tested first, then take a supplement on your doctor's advice.

Why am I tired even after sleeping?

There are many reasons: light sleep from evening coffee or alcohol, an irregular routine, sleep apnoea, anaemia, thyroid problems or depression. If you keep waking up tired for weeks, see a doctor.

How much coffee a day is safe?

According to EFSA, up to 400 mg of caffeine a day for healthy adults, roughly 3–⁠4 cups of filter coffee. Up to 200 mg in pregnancy.

Can I give up coffee abruptly?

You can, but the first few days may bring headaches, drowsiness and irritability. It is easier to cut back by half a cup every few days.
