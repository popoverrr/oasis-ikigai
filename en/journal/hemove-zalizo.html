Heme iron: how it differs from regular iron

Published 8 October 2026 · Updated 8 October 2026 · 6 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Heme iron is the form found in meat and fish, and it is absorbed better than plant iron. But any iron should only be taken when tests show you are low. Here is why.

How does heme iron differ from regular iron? Heme iron is the form bound to heme, as in meat, poultry and fish; it is absorbed better and depends less on what you take it with. Non-heme iron, from plants and most supplements, is absorbed less well. But the main rule is the same for both: iron is taken only after blood tests, because too much is harmful.

Heme and non-heme iron

Dietary iron comes in two forms: - heme iron — part of haemoglobin and myoglobin; found in red meat, liver, poultry, fish and seafood; - non-heme iron — in legumes, grains, spinach, nuts, fortified foods and most iron supplements (ferrous sulfate, fumarate, gluconate and so on).

Most people's diets contain more non-heme iron, but heme iron contributes noticeably because it is absorbed better. The body uses iron to make haemoglobin, the red blood cell protein that carries oxygen, and myoglobin in muscles. So low iron eventually shows up as tiredness, weakness, breathlessness on exertion and pallor.

Absorption: what is known

According to NIH, overall iron bioavailability is about 14–⁠18% from mixed diets and 5–⁠12% from vegetarian diets. The difference is largely because heme iron is absorbed more efficiently than non-heme iron.

What you eat with non-heme iron matters a lot: - improve absorption — vitamin C (fruit, vegetables, juice) and meat, fish or poultry in the same meal; - reduce absorption — calcium, coffee and tea (polyphenols), and phytates from bran and whole grains.

Heme iron depends less on these factors, which is why people who find it hard to track combinations choose it. Still, keep calcium separate from it. More in Which vitamins and minerals should not be taken together.

The body also regulates iron absorption itself: when stores are low it absorbs more, and when they are high, less. But this protection does not work in hereditary haemochromatosis and does not prevent overdose from supplements.

Folic acid and B12 in the formula

Making healthy red blood cells takes more than iron. Folic acid and vitamin B12 are involved in red blood cell maturation, and a lack of them can also cause anaemia, of a different type. That is why “iron + folic acid + B12” combinations are common.

If your tiredness is caused by low B12 or folate rather than iron, a combined product can partly mask the picture — another reason to start with tests rather than a supplement. Vitamin B12 matters especially for vegans and older people, who absorb it less well from food.

Who it suits and who it does not

Low iron is more common in: - women of reproductive age, because of monthly blood loss — on women's basic needs, see Vitamins for women; - pregnant women — the requirement rises to 27 mg a day (NIH), but iron during pregnancy is prescribed by a doctor; - frequent blood donors; - vegetarians and vegans; - people with gut conditions that impair absorption.

NIH recommended intakes are 8 mg a day for men and for women over 50, and 18 mg for women aged 19–⁠50. The upper limit for adults is 45 mg a day.

Who should not take iron without a doctor: people with hereditary haemochromatosis or other iron-overload conditions, people with some anaemias not caused by iron deficiency, and children. NIH specifically warns that accidental swallowing of iron supplements is a dangerous poisoning risk for young children, so keep them out of reach.

Tests. The most informative measure of iron stores is ferritin, together with a full blood count (haemoglobin). The decision on whether to take iron, the dose and the duration is your doctor's.

How to take it and what not to combine it with

If your doctor recommends iron: - take it with food if it causes nausea or stomach pain on an empty stomach; - separate it from calcium, dairy, coffee and tea by at least 1–⁠2 hours; - keep it apart from thyroid medicines, some antibiotics and acid-reducing drugs; - take it with water.

Constipation, dark stools and nausea are common side effects of iron supplements, especially at high doses. If you get abdominal pain or other worrying signs, see a doctor. How to spread supplements through the day: When to take vitamins: morning or evening.

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Take iron supplements only after blood tests (ferritin, full blood count) and on a doctor's recommendation: too much iron is harmful. Keep iron supplements out of reach of children.

FAQ

Does heme iron cause constipation?

Constipation and nausea are common side effects of iron supplements, especially at high doses. A moderate dose taken with food reduces discomfort. If problems persist, talk to your doctor.

Can I take iron with coffee?

No, coffee and tea reduce iron absorption. Take iron at least 1–⁠2 hours after them.

How long should I take heme iron?

It depends on how low your stores were. Your doctor decides based on tests, usually with a repeat ferritin test after several weeks.

Can I take iron without tests, as prevention?

Better not. Excess iron is harmful, and tiredness can have other causes, from low B12 to thyroid problems.

Why is heme iron better than regular iron?

It is absorbed better and depends less on food. But to treat a confirmed deficiency, a doctor may also choose a non-heme form, in different doses and regimens.
