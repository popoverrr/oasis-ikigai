Sleep hygiene: 10 rules for better rest

Published 22 October 2026 · Updated 22 October 2026 · 8 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Good sleep starts long before you get into bed. Here are 10 sleep hygiene rules with the best evidence, what is known about melatonin and supplements, and when insomnia is a reason to see a doctor.

To sleep better, start with a fixed wake-up time, morning light, a cool dark bedroom and a calm hour before bed without coffee, alcohol or bright screens. These sleep hygiene rules have the best evidence and work for most people. Supplements can be an add-on, but if insomnia lasts for months, you need a doctor, not another jar.

Why sleep is the foundation

During sleep the brain processes and consolidates the day's information, while the body repairs tissues, regulates appetite and stress hormones and tunes the immune response. Chronic sleep loss is linked to poorer memory and mood, increased appetite, more frequent colds, weight gain and high blood pressure.

How much you need: 7–⁠9 hours a night for adults, and at least 7 for most people. Older adults sometimes need a little less, but not dramatically less. The test is simple: you wake up without heaviness, do not nod off in meetings or at the wheel, and do not “catch up” by sleeping three extra hours at weekends.

Sleep hygiene is a set of habits that help the brain understand that it is time to sleep. Below are 10 rules grouped by topic.

Routine and light

Rule 1. Get up at the same time every day. Even at weekends, within an hour. A fixed wake-up time is the strongest signal for your internal clock; bedtime will follow.

Rule 2. Catch the morning light. 15–⁠30 minutes of daylight in the first hour after waking — a walk, coffee on the balcony, walking to work. Morning light “starts” the day and helps you feel sleepy on time in the evening. In winter, when it gets light late, go outside at least at lunchtime.

Rule 3. Go to bed when you are sleepy, not when it is “time”. If you cannot fall asleep in about 20 minutes, get up, go to another room with dim light and do something calm (a paper book, quiet music). Return to bed when you feel sleepy. This way the bed becomes linked with sleep, not tossing and turning.

Daytime naps should be no longer than 20–⁠30 minutes and no later than mid-afternoon. A long afternoon nap steals sleep from the night.

The bedroom: temperature, quiet, darkness

Rule 4. A cool bedroom. The body falls asleep more easily as its temperature drops slightly. Around 18 °C is comfortable for most people. During the heating season, air the room before bed and do not overheat it.

Rule 5. Darkness and quiet. Blackout curtains, a sleep mask, no LED lights on devices. If noise is a problem, try earplugs or steady white noise. During power cuts and air-raid alerts it is hard to rest in the dark; a routine, a warm-toned torch and a prepared spot so you do not have to search for things at night all help.

The bed is for sleep only. Do not work, watch series or scroll the news in bed. That way the brain learns: bed = sleep.

Coffee, alcohol and dinner

Rule 6. Coffee before lunch. Caffeine acts for hours: even if you fall asleep after an evening coffee, your sleep becomes lighter. It is safer to keep coffee, strong tea, energy drinks and cola to the first half of the day. For keeping your energy up without extra cups, see Energy without extra coffee: 7 daily habits.

Rule 7. Alcohol does not help you sleep. It may help you drop off faster, but in the second half of the night sleep becomes broken and you wake more often. If you drink, do so early and in moderation.

Rule 8. A light dinner 2–⁠3 hours before bed. A heavy, fatty or spicy meal late in the evening makes it harder to fall asleep — and so does hunger. If you are hungry, try yoghurt, a banana or a handful of nuts. Lots of fluid before bed means night-time trips to the toilet.

Moving during the day improves sleep, but intense exercise late in the evening keeps some people awake, so finish a few hours before bed.

Devices before bed

Rule 9. An hour without bright screens. It is not only about blue light: social media, news and work emails stimulate the brain and switch on anxiety. Night mode is better than nothing but does not cancel this effect.

What to do instead:

  • charge your phone outside the bedroom and use an ordinary alarm clock;
  • choose a “closing the day” ritual — a warm shower or bath, stretching, a paper book, quiet music;
  • write down your thoughts — if your to-do list keeps spinning, put it on paper so you can let it go until morning.

Rule 10. Keep the same ritual every evening. The brain loves predictability: the same 20–⁠30 minutes of calm activities before bed become a signal that it is time to rest. The Japanese tradition of an evening ofuro bath is a good example of such a ritual.

Supplements to discuss with your doctor

Supplements do not replace the rules above, but they are sometimes discussed as an add-on.

Melatonin. A hormone the body produces in the dark. According to NCCIH, melatonin may help with jet lag and some circadian rhythm sleep disorders; for ordinary insomnia the effect is small. Long-term safety is not well studied and melatonin can interact with medicines. Take it only after talking to your doctor.

Magnesium. This mineral is needed for the nervous system and muscles, but there is little convincing evidence that it improves sleep in people who are not deficient. More in Magnesium and sleep: what the science says.

GABA. Studies are small; more in GABA supplements: what they are and what is known.

When insomnia means seeing a doctor

A bad night after a hard day is normal. See a doctor if:

  • trouble falling asleep or frequent waking happens 3 or more nights a week for several weeks;
  • you snore and others notice pauses in your breathing, or you wake with a headache and a dry mouth — possible signs of sleep apnoea;
  • you fall asleep during the day at unexpected moments, especially while driving;
  • in the evening you get an unpleasant feeling in your legs and an urge to move them;
  • insomnia comes with anxiety, low mood or pain;
  • you regularly use sleeping pills or alcohol to fall asleep.

Chronic insomnia is very treatable: the first-line treatment doctors recommend is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), a short course that changes the habits and thoughts that keep insomnia going. On the link between sleep and stress, see Stress and fatigue: daily habits and Japanese supplements.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines and do not treat insomnia. Seedcoms GABA contains St John's wort, which interacts with many medicines, including antidepressants, contraceptives and anticoagulants. Snoring with breathing pauses, falling asleep during the day and insomnia lasting weeks are reasons to see a doctor.

FAQ

How many hours of sleep does an adult need?

7–⁠9 hours a night, and at least 7 for most adults. Go by how you feel during the day: if you wake refreshed and do not doze off in the daytime, you are getting enough.

Can I drink coffee in the afternoon?

Better not: caffeine acts for hours and makes sleep lighter, even if you do fall asleep. Keep coffee, strong tea and energy drinks to the first half of the day.

Does melatonin help?

According to NCCIH, melatonin may help with jet lag and some circadian rhythm disorders, while for ordinary insomnia the effect is small. Take it only after talking to your doctor.

What should I do if I wake at 3–4 am?

Do not check the time or pick up your phone. If you have not fallen back asleep in about 20 minutes, get up and do something calm in dim light. If this happens for weeks, see a doctor.

Is it bad to catch up on sleep at weekends?

A big shift in your schedule at weekends throws off your internal clock and makes Monday mornings harder. It is better to get up at roughly the same time and take a short nap if needed.
