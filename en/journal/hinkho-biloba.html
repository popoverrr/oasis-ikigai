Ginkgo biloba: what is known and what not to combine it with

Published 22 October 2026 · Updated 22 October 2026 · 7 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Ginkgo is one of the most popular herbal supplements for memory, and also one where knowing the interactions matters most. What large trials showed and who should not take ginkgo.

Ginkgo biloba is an extract from the leaves of an ancient tree, popular as a “memory” supplement, but large randomised trials have not shown that it prevents dementia or age-related memory decline. The key thing to know before taking it: ginkgo may increase the risk of bleeding, so it is not combined with anticoagulants or aspirin and not taken before surgery without a doctor's approval.

The ginkgo tree and its history

Ginkgo biloba is one of the oldest trees on Earth: its relatives grew in the age of the dinosaurs, which is why it is often called a “living fossil”. The tree is native to China but is most associated with Japan: ginkgo (ichō) grows by temples and along Tokyo's streets, and its golden autumn leaves are one of the city's symbols.

The tree is remarkably hardy: six ginkgo trees survived the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and kept growing. That story made ginkgo a symbol of resilience.

Traditional Chinese practice mainly used ginkgo seeds, while modern supplements are made from the leaves. Important: raw ginkgo seeds are toxic and must not be eaten; supplements use leaf extract.

The EGb extract and standardisation

Ginkgo leaves on their own are not a supplement. Research has most often used the standardised extract EGb 761, in which two groups of compounds are controlled:

  • flavonoid glycosides — about 24%;
  • terpene lactones (ginkgolides and bilobalide) — about 6%;
  • ginkgolic acids — kept to a minimum because they can cause allergic reactions.

Why this matters: results from studies of one extract cannot automatically be applied to any ginkgo leaf powder. A good-quality supplement states that it uses an extract rather than ground leaves.

What the research shows

Ginkgo was long considered promising for memory and circulation. Large studies gave cautious answers.

  • GEM (Ginkgo Evaluation of Memory) — one of the largest trials of a herbal supplement: more than 3,000 people aged 75 and over took 240 mg of ginkgo extract a day or a placebo for about six years on average. Ginkgo did not reduce the rate of dementia or Alzheimer's disease (JAMA, 2008).
  • A further analysis of the same trial (JAMA, 2009) found that ginkgo did not slow age-related decline in memory, attention or thinking.
  • NCCIH concludes that there is no conclusive evidence that ginkgo helps with any condition; studies of tinnitus, leg circulation problems and other conditions have produced mixed results.

In practice, this means ginkgo is not a “memory pill”. If you notice your memory getting worse, see a doctor: the cause may be poor sleep, stress, depression, low vitamin B12, thyroid problems or blood pressure. For what genuinely helps memory, read Memory and focus: sleep, nutrition and supplements.

Interactions with medicines

This is the most important section. Ginkgo may affect blood clotting, so:

Medicine or situationWhy caution is needed
Anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, etc.)Possible increased risk of bleeding
Aspirin, clopidogrel and other antiplatelet drugsThe same
Regular NSAID painkillers (ibuprofen, diclofenac)Combined effect on bleeding
Planned surgery or dental proceduresGinkgo is stopped in advance — your doctor will say when
Epilepsy medicinesSeizures have been reported; raw ginkgo seeds can trigger them
Diabetes medicines, some antidepressantsPossible interactions — only with your doctor

Other supplements with a similar effect on clotting include high-dose fish oil, high-dose vitamin E and garlic capsules. Combining them with ginkgo should also be discussed with a doctor. General rules are in Which vitamins and minerals should not be taken together.

Possible side effects of ginkgo include headache, dizziness, stomach upset, constipation and allergic skin reactions. If you notice unexplained bruising, bleeding gums or nosebleeds, stop taking it and see a doctor.

Who should not take it

Do not take ginkgo without your doctor's approval if you:

  • take anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs (including daily aspirin) or regular NSAIDs;
  • have a bleeding disorder or have had bleeding problems;
  • have surgery or a dental procedure planned;
  • have epilepsy or have had seizures;
  • are pregnant or breastfeeding — there are not enough safety data;
  • have diabetes and take blood-sugar-lowering medicines;
  • are allergic to ginkgo.

Ginkgo supplements are not intended for children.

If your goal is to support your brain and eyes as you age, remember the basics: sleep, movement, social contact, learning new skills and a diet with fish and vegetables. For fats that matter for the brain and retina, see Omega‑3 for eyes and brain: what is known.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines and do not prevent dementia. Ginkgo may increase the risk of bleeding: if you take anticoagulants, aspirin or other antiplatelet drugs, before surgery and dental procedures, with epilepsy, and during pregnancy or breastfeeding, take it only with your doctor's approval.

FAQ

Does ginkgo improve memory?

Large randomised trials have not confirmed this: in the GEM trial, ginkgo did not prevent dementia or slow age-related memory decline. If your memory has worsened, see a doctor to find the cause.

How much ginkgo biloba should I take?

Follow the directions on the pack: for ORIHIRO, 10 tablets a day in several doses, over a 3-month course. Do not exceed the dose or combine several ginkgo products.

Can I take ginkgo with aspirin?

Not without your doctor's approval. Both affect blood clotting, so combining them may increase the risk of bleeding.

When should I stop ginkgo before surgery?

Tell your surgeon and anaesthetist that you take ginkgo at the planning stage. Your doctor will decide when to stop.

Can I eat ginkgo seeds?

Raw seeds are toxic. Supplements use leaf extract; the seeds appear as food only in traditional dishes, after processing and in small amounts.
