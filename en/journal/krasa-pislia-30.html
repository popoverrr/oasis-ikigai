Skin care after 30: what to add to your routine and diet

Published 15 October 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026 · 6 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

After 30, skin recovers more slowly and dries out more often. Here is what is really worth adding to your routine, how to start retinol and the role of diet and supplements.

What should you add to your routine after 30? The foundation is three steps: gentle cleansing, moisturising and sunscreen every day. Next comes retinol in the evening, introduced gradually, and a ceramide cream for dry skin if needed. Diet, sleep and supplements such as ceramides or elastin complement these steps but do not replace them.

What changes in skin after 30

Thirty is not a sharp line, but many people notice changes: skin recovers more slowly and marks from breakouts linger; the first fine lines appear around the eyes and on the forehead; skin gets drier, especially in winter; the effects of sun — pigmentation and uneven tone — become more visible; and tiredness shows on the face faster.

These are natural processes, and the aim of skincare is not to stop time but to keep skin healthy, hydrated and protected. Many changes we put down to age are actually caused by the sun, which is why the most important step starts there.

The basics: cleanse, moisturise, SPF

Cleanse twice a day with a gentle product; the American Academy of Dermatology recommends lukewarm rather than hot water and not scrubbing. If your skin feels tight after washing, the cleanser is too strong.

Moisturise right after cleansing while skin is slightly damp: ceramides and hyaluronic acid for dry skin, light gels for oily skin. The Japanese approach adds a lotion (化粧水) between cleansing and cream.

SPF every day. A broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher is the most important anti-ageing product and the one most often skipped. Apply it every morning, even in winter and on cloudy days.

These three steps alone can noticeably improve your skin within a few months.

Retinol: when and how to start

Retinol is one of the best-studied ingredients for fine lines and uneven tone. The rules: start slowly — 2–⁠3 evenings a week, a pea-sized amount; evenings only, with SPF in the daytime; not on the same evening as acids and scrubs; and not during pregnancy or breastfeeding — the NHS warns about retinoids at this time.

A step-by-step plan is in Japanese retinol: how to start without irritation.

Diet and supplements for skin

  • Protein — the building material for collagen and elastin.
  • Vitamin C — needed to make collagen; berries, citrus, peppers, cabbage.
  • Fats — omega‑3 from fish, oils and nuts; see Omega‑3: how to choose a good one.
  • Water — dehydration shows on the skin.

“Skin” supplements — ceramides, collagen, elastin — can be added as part of a “beauty from within” ritual, but there is less research on them than on creams and SPF, and the effect is gradual. See Ceramide supplements and Elastin supplements.

Sleep, stress and skin

Lack of sleep and chronic stress impair barrier repair, can trigger breakouts and make dark circles and puffiness more visible. What helps: 7–⁠9 hours of sleep, a consistent bedtime, a screen-free evening ritual and daytime movement. On evening rituals, see How to build a daily wellness ritual.

What to avoid

Too many actives at once — acids, retinol, vitamin C and scrubs on the same evening dry out and irritate the skin; switching products often — skin needs several weeks to show a response; skipping SPF; and promises of “ten years younger” — marketing not worth believing.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace advice from a dermatologist. Cosmetics and food supplements do not treat skin conditions. Retinol products are not used during pregnancy or breastfeeding. If you have breakouts, rapidly changing pigmentation or irritation that does not settle, see a doctor.

FAQ

At what age should I start retinol?

There is no fixed age; many people add retinol after 25–⁠30 when the first fine lines appear. Start slowly and always use SPF in the daytime.

Do I need skin supplements?

Not necessarily. The basics are cleansing, moisturising and SPF. Supplements can be part of a ritual, but their effect is gradual and less studied.

What matters more: cream or diet?

Both. But daily sunscreen has the biggest effect on how skin looks, while diet provides its building materials.

Do I need a separate eye cream?

Not necessarily, but the skin around the eyes is thinner and more sensitive, so a light, non-irritating product is handy.

How often should I change products?

No more than once every few weeks, so you can judge how your skin responds.
