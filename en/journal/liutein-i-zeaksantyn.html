Lutein and zeaxanthin: how they work for the eyes

Published 8 October 2026 · Updated 8 October 2026 · 7 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Lutein and zeaxanthin are yellow pigments that build up in the centre of the retina. Here is what research says about them, how much is in food and when a supplement makes sense.

How do lutein and zeaxanthin work for the eyes? They are yellow carotenoids that accumulate in the macula, the central part of the retina, and form the macular pigment. It absorbs some blue light and has antioxidant properties. Their role is best studied in people with age-related macular degeneration: in the AREDS2 study, a formula with 10 mg lutein and 2 mg zeaxanthin became part of the recommended supplement.

What macular pigment is

The macula is a small area in the centre of the retina responsible for sharp central vision — reading, recognising faces, fine detail. Lutein, zeaxanthin and their relative meso-zeaxanthin are concentrated there, giving the macula its yellowish tint (hence “macula lutea”, the yellow spot).

What is known about macular pigment: - it filters blue light — part of the short-wavelength light reaching the retina; - it acts as an antioxidant — the retina uses a lot of oxygen and receives a lot of light, so protection from oxidative stress matters; - it may affect visual quality — contrast and recovery from glare; the evidence here is less clear-cut.

The body cannot make lutein and zeaxanthin; they come only from food or supplements.

Lutein and zeaxanthin in food

Dark green leafy vegetables are the richest sources of lutein: kale, spinach and collard greens, as well as broccoli, peas and courgettes. Zeaxanthin is found in sweetcorn, orange peppers and saffron. Egg yolk is a special source: it contains less lutein, but its fat helps absorption.

Practical tips: eat spinach, broccoli and peas several times a week; cook vegetables with a little oil, since carotenoids are fat-soluble; eggs, sweetcorn and yellow or orange peppers are easy sources of zeaxanthin.

There is no official recommended daily intake for lutein and zeaxanthin.

What the AREDS2 study showed

AREDS and AREDS2 are large studies by the US National Eye Institute (NEI) in people with age-related macular degeneration (AMD). In the first AREDS, a formula of vitamins C and E, beta-carotene, zinc and copper reduced the risk of progression to late AMD in people with intermediate AMD. In AREDS2, beta-carotene was replaced with 10 mg lutein and 2 mg zeaxanthin: the formula was just as effective and safer for smokers, in whom beta-carotene had been linked to a higher risk of lung cancer.

Key points from NEI: - the AREDS2 formula is recommended for people with intermediate AMD or late AMD in one eye, not for everyone as prevention; - it neither restores lost vision nor treats AMD; it lowers the risk of progression; - there is no evidence of benefit in people without AMD or with early AMD.

For healthy people who spend long hours at a screen, a supplement is a way to complement a diet low in green vegetables, not a remedy for tired eyes.

Dosage and duration

  • The AREDS2 formula provides 10 mg lutein and 2 mg zeaxanthin a day.
  • Everyday supplements usually contain lower or comparable amounts.
  • Lutein accumulates in the retina gradually, so its effect is judged over months.

Take it with food that contains fat. No serious side effects are known at usual doses; very high carotenoid intake can give the skin a yellowish tinge that fades when the dose is reduced.

On combining it with other supplements, see When to take vitamins: morning or evening. Omega‑3 DHA is also part of the retina: Omega‑3: how to choose a good one.

When to see an ophthalmologist

Supplements do not replace an eye exam. See an ophthalmologist if straight lines look wavy or a grey or blurry spot appears in the centre of your vision; your vision gets worse suddenly; you are over 50 and have not had your retina checked for a long time; AMD or glaucoma runs in your family; or your eyes are red, painful or watery for more than two days.

The World Health Organization stresses that much vision impairment can be prevented or corrected with timely care. On habits that protect eyes, see Eyes and screens: protecting a schoolchild's eyesight.

FAQ

How much lutein a day?

There is no official recommendation. AREDS2 used 10 mg lutein and 2 mg zeaxanthin a day in people with age-related macular degeneration. For everyday use, follow the maker's serving.

Can children take lutein?

The best lutein sources for a child are green vegetables and eggs. Supplements for children should be prescribed by a paediatrician or ophthalmologist.

When does lutein start to work?

Lutein builds up gradually and its effect is judged over months. Do not expect an instant effect as with eye drops.

Does lutein help with eye strain at the computer?

Screen-related eye strain is mainly due to blinking less and the effort of focusing. Breaks following the 20-20-20 rule and lubricating drops help most.

Should I take lutein with food?

Yes, with a meal that contains fat, because lutein is fat-soluble.
