Magnesium and sleep: what science says

Published 15 October 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026 · 6 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Magnesium is often recommended “for sleep”, but research results are modest. Here is what is actually known, who magnesium might help and what works better for sleep than any supplement.

Does magnesium help you sleep? The evidence is modest: small studies suggest magnesium supplements may slightly improve sleep, mainly in people whose diet is short of magnesium, such as older adults. For most healthy people, do not expect a bigger effect than from a sleep schedule, daylight and an evening ritual. As part of such a ritual it is reasonable and safe within 350 mg a day from supplements.

How magnesium relates to the nervous system

Magnesium is involved in hundreds of reactions, and its role in the nervous system is particularly visible. It regulates NMDA receptors, through which excitatory signals pass; it is involved in the GABA system, the brain's main inhibitory system; it is needed for muscles to relax after contracting; and it takes part in cellular energy metabolism.

So if magnesium is low, the nervous system may be more excitable and muscles tenser — which is why low magnesium is linked to tiredness, irritability, cramps and sometimes poor sleep. But “a little too low is bad” does not mean “more is better”: when you have enough, extra magnesium does not switch your brain off at night.

On forms, intakes and the upper limit, see Magnesium: which form to choose and when to take it.

What studies show about sleep

  • Most studies are small — dozens of participants, short durations, varying forms and doses.
  • Most data come from older adults, who often sleep worse and get less magnesium; some of these studies found moderate improvements in falling asleep and sleep duration.
  • In young healthy people there is no convincing evidence of a substantial effect.
  • Reviews generally conclude that the quality of evidence is low.

NCCIH stresses that for chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the most effective approach — not supplements. Magnesium is worth trying as part of an evening ritual, especially if your diet is low in nuts, legumes, grains and greens.

Magnesium and GABA: different mechanisms

Magnesium is an essential mineral with a proven nutritional role, recommended intakes and an upper limit. GABA is a neurotransmitter the body makes itself; dietary GABA is added to evening supplements, but its effects are even less studied. You can combine them, but read the label: some GABA formulas contain St John's wort, which interacts with many medicines. See GABA supplements: what they are and what is known.

An evening ritual for better sleep

Habits have the best evidence:

  1. The same wake-up and bed time, even at weekends.
  2. Morning daylight — 15–⁠30 minutes outdoors.
  3. Less caffeine after lunch.
  4. No heavy dinner or alcohol before bed — alcohol worsens sleep quality.
  5. A warm bath or shower 1–⁠2 hours before bed.
  6. Dim light and no screens in the last hour.
  7. A cool, dark, quiet bedroom.
  8. Bed only for sleep — if you cannot sleep after 20 minutes, get up and do something calm.

Magnesium is a detail that is easy to take with dinner. On the Japanese approach to evenings, see How to build a daily wellness ritual.

When the problem is not magnesium

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if insomnia lasts more than three months or affects daily life. Causes include sleep apnoea, restless legs syndrome (sometimes linked to low iron), anxiety and depression, thyroid problems, pain, night-time urination or hot flushes, medicines and shift work. A supplement will not solve these.

If insomnia lasts more than three months or comes with snoring and breathing pauses or low mood, see a doctor. If you have kidney disease or take medication, talk to your doctor first.

FAQ

How long before bed should I take magnesium?

There is no strict rule. Taking it with dinner or 1–⁠2 hours before bed works well as part of a routine.

Can I take magnesium and GABA together?

Yes. If the GABA formula contains St John's wort and you take medicines, talk to a doctor first.

Does magnesium cause daytime drowsiness?

At normal doses magnesium does not act as a sleeping pill and does not usually cause daytime drowsiness.

Will magnesium help with insomnia?

Studies show a modest effect, mainly in people low in magnesium. For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy works best.

How long should I take magnesium for sleep?

Judge the effect over 2–⁠4 weeks. If nothing changes, there is no point continuing — see a doctor instead.
