Omega‑3 for eyes and brain: what is known

Published 19 October 2026 · Updated 19 October 2026 · 7 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

DHA is one of the main structural fats of the retina and brain. What science really knows about omega‑3 for the eyes, why the dry eye findings are mixed, and how to combine fish, lutein and supplements.

Omega‑3 for the eyes mainly means DHA (docosahexaenoic acid), which makes up a large share of the fats in the retina and brain. It is needed for the normal structure of these tissues, but in studies the effect of omega‑3 supplements on adult eyesight and dry eye has been more modest than advertising suggests. The most reliable strategy is to eat oily fish regularly and use a supplement as a top-up when you eat little fish.

DHA in the retina and brain

Omega‑3s are a family of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Three matter most for people:

  • ALA (alpha-linolenic acid) — from plants: flaxseed, walnuts, rapeseed oil;
  • EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) — from fish and seafood;
  • DHA (docosahexaenoic acid) — from fish, seafood and algae.

The body converts only a small amount of ALA into EPA and DHA, so marine sources matter.

DHA is concentrated in the retina's photoreceptors — the cells that detect light — and in the brain's grey matter. It keeps cell membranes flexible, which matters for passing signals. That is why DHA is added to infant formula and recommended in pregnancy: according to NIH, it is important for the development of a baby's brain and eyes.

In adults the picture is different: DHA deficiency severe enough to affect vision is rare on a normal diet. So the question is not whether the eyes need DHA (they do) but whether extra DHA from capsules brings a noticeable benefit to someone who already eats fish. Here the answers are more cautious.

To learn how to read a label and what the EPA, DHA and DPA figures mean, see Omega‑3: how to choose a good one.

Omega-3 and dry eye: what studies show

Dry eye is one of the most common complaints at the eye doctor: burning, a gritty feeling, redness and tired eyes by evening. Omega‑3s were long seen as promising support because they influence inflammation and the quality of the tear film's oily layer.

What the research found:

  • DREAM — a large trial funded by the US National Eye Institute (NEI). People with moderate to severe dry eye took 3 g of omega‑3 a day (EPA + DHA) or an olive oil placebo for a year. Symptoms improved equally in both groups — omega‑3 was no better than placebo.
  • A Cochrane review (2019) pooled dozens of studies and concluded that evidence on omega‑3 for dry eye is inconsistent and of low certainty.
  • AREDS2 — another NEI study — found that adding omega‑3 to an eye formula did not reduce the risk of age-related macular degeneration getting worse.

The honest conclusion: omega‑3 is a useful part of a healthy diet, but not a remedy for dry eye. If your eyes are constantly dry, see an eye doctor: the cause may be eyelid problems, hormones, medicines, contact lenses or autoimmune conditions. To ease symptoms, doctors usually suggest lubricating drops — Japanese options are covered in Japanese eye drops: how to choose.

Omega-3 and screen work

Eye strain from screens is mainly about blinking and focusing: at a monitor we blink less, tears evaporate, and the muscles that focus the lens work at close range for hours. No supplement replaces breaks and a well-set-up desk.

What actually works:

  • the 20-20-20 rule — every 20 minutes, look at something 6 metres (20 feet) away for 20 seconds;
  • deliberate blinking — a few full blinks during each break;
  • a screen slightly below eye level — your eyes open less and dry out less;
  • humidifying the air during the heating season.

Omega‑3 is part of your overall diet here, not a “pill for programmers”. More practical tips in Digital eye strain: the 20-20-20 rule and care.

Lutein as a partner

If omega‑3 is a building block of membranes, lutein and zeaxanthin are the pigments of the macula, the central part of the retina. They absorb some blue light and act as antioxidants. In AREDS2, a formula with lutein and zeaxanthin (plus vitamins C and E, zinc and copper) lowered the risk of intermediate age-related macular degeneration progressing. That finding applies to a specific group of people with the diagnosis, not to everyone.

Omega‑3 and lutein do not compete; they complement each other and can be taken together. Lutein is fat-soluble, so it is absorbed better with a meal containing fat, and it pairs conveniently with an omega‑3 capsule. For more on the macular pigments, read Lutein and zeaxanthin: how they work for the eyes.

Eating for your eyes

The best-studied “food for the eyes” is simply a varied diet with fish and vegetables.

  • Oily fish once or twice a week — salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines, trout. Many dietary guidelines recommend this, and it provides EPA and DHA along with vitamin D and protein.
  • Dark leafy greens — spinach, kale, broccoli — sources of lutein.
  • Eggs — the yolk contains well-absorbed lutein and zeaxanthin.
  • Orange and yellow vegetables and fruit — carrots, pumpkin, sweetcorn, peppers — beta-carotene and zeaxanthin.
  • Nuts and seeds — walnuts, flax, chia — plant omega‑3 (ALA) and vitamin E.

The same rules apply to the brain: fish, vegetables, pulses, whole grains and as few ultra-processed foods as possible. For memory and focus specifically, see Memory and focus: sleep, nutrition and supplements.

How to choose supplements

An omega‑3 supplement makes sense if you rarely eat fish; lutein, if your diet is low in greens and eggs or if an eye doctor has suggested it for retinal changes.

Things to keep in mind:

  • total EPA + DHA — EFSA considers up to 5 g a day safe for adults; ordinary supplements provide far less;
  • anticoagulants — if you take warfarin, clopidogrel or other medicines that affect blood clotting, discuss omega‑3 with your doctor;
  • surgery — before a planned operation, tell your doctor which supplements you take;
  • fish allergy — fish-based supplements are not suitable;
  • storage — somewhere cool and out of the sun; do not use capsules with a strong rancid smell.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines. Persistent dry eyes, worsening vision, spots or wavy lines in your vision are reasons to see an eye doctor. If you take anticoagulants, are having surgery, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, take omega‑3 only after talking to your doctor.

FAQ

Does omega-3 help with dry eyes?

The evidence is mixed: in the large DREAM trial omega‑3 did no better than placebo. Omega‑3 is good as part of your diet, but constant dry eye needs an eye examination and, most often, lubricating drops.

Can I take omega-3 with lutein?

Yes, they complement each other, and lutein is absorbed better with fat. Take both with food and do not exceed the dose on the pack.

Which fish is best for the eyes?

Oily sea fish: salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout. One or two portions a week is enough.

How long should I take omega-3?

The manufacturer recommends a 3-month course. Omega‑3 is part of nutrition rather than a quick fix, so judge how you feel after several weeks, not days.

Can I get DHA without fish?

Yes, from supplements based on microalgae oil. Plant ALA from flax and walnuts converts to DHA only in small amounts.
