Memory and focus: sleep, nutrition and supplements

Published 22 October 2026 · Updated 22 October 2026 · 8 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Forgot why you walked into the room, and cannot finish a page without checking your phone? What really affects memory and focus, and what to expect — and not expect — from vitamins for the brain.

Memory and focus are improved most not by vitamins but by sleep, movement, a diet with fish and vegetables, and the habit of working on one thing at a time without constant notifications. In large trials, omega‑3 and ginkgo supplements did not improve memory in healthy adults, so they can only complement your diet. On the other hand, low vitamin B12, low iron or thyroid problems really do affect attention, and they are worth checking.

What affects concentration

Attention is a limited resource, and dozens of small things eat into it every day. The most common reasons it is hard to focus:

  • lack of sleep — even one short night noticeably worsens attention and reaction time;
  • constant switching — every notification, email or message pulls you out of a task, and getting back into it takes time;
  • stress and anxiety — the brain is busy scanning for threats rather than reading the text in front of you;
  • hunger and sharp swings in blood sugar — energy drops quickly after a sugary snack;
  • dehydration and stuffy air — a warm, dry room that is never aired;
  • sitting still — hours at a desk without breaks;
  • alcohol — especially regular drinking.

There are also medical causes worth ruling out if the problem lasts for weeks:

  • anaemia and iron deficiency — tiredness and “brain fog”; see Iron deficiency in women: signs, tests and what to do;
  • low vitamin B12 — more common in vegans, older adults and people taking metformin or acid-reducing medicines;
  • thyroid problems;
  • depression — which shows up not only as sadness but also as poor concentration and memory;
  • side effects of medicines — some sleeping pills, antihistamines and sedatives.

Sleep and memory

Sleep is not a pause in brain activity but the time when the brain sorts and consolidates everything you learned during the day. During deep sleep, new memories move into long-term storage, and during REM sleep the brain links them with what you already know.

What follows from this:

  • an all-nighter is worse than sleep — if you are preparing for an exam or an important meeting, sleeping the night before helps more than two extra hours of revision;
  • a steady routine — 7–⁠9 hours and a fixed wake-up time give the brain a predictable rhythm;
  • a short nap — 20 minutes during the day can refresh your attention, but a long afternoon nap disrupts night-time sleep.

Ten simple rules for good sleep are in Sleep hygiene: 10 rules for better rest.

Eating for the brain

The brain is one of the most energy-hungry organs, and it needs steady nutrition rather than a single “superfood”.

What is known:

  • regular meals with protein and fibre keep energy steady without sharp dips;
  • oily fish once or twice a week provides DHA, one of the brain's main fats;
  • vegetables, especially leafy greens, and berries — in observational studies, Mediterranean-style diets are linked to slower memory decline with age;
  • nuts, olive oil, pulses and whole grains form the base of those same diets;
  • water — even mild dehydration impairs attention;
  • coffee in moderation can perk you up, but after lunch it disturbs sleep and therefore next-day memory. For energy without extra cups, see Energy without extra coffee: 7 daily habits.

In its guidelines on reducing the risk of cognitive decline, WHO recommends a balanced diet but does not recommend vitamins, omega‑3 or herbal supplements for this purpose unless there is a deficiency.

Omega-3 and ginkgo: what is known

The two most popular “brain” supplements are omega‑3 and ginkgo biloba. What large studies found:

  • omega‑3 — in the AREDS2 trial (JAMA, 2015), older adults took omega‑3 (DHA + EPA) or a placebo for several years. There was no difference in the decline of memory and thinking. At the same time, DHA matters for a child's brain development, and oily fish remains a useful part of an adult diet;
  • ginkgo — in the GEM trial, more than 3,000 people aged 75+ took ginkgo extract for several years. It did not reduce the rate of dementia or slow memory decline.

The honest conclusion: if you eat well, supplements are unlikely to noticeably improve your memory. They may make sense when you eat little fish — and as part of a broader self-care ritual rather than a “focus pill”.

Habits for focus

Things you can change today:

  • one task at a time — close extra tabs and turn off notifications while you work;
  • work in blocks — 25–⁠50 minutes of focused work and a 5–⁠10 minute break (the Pomodoro method is one version);
  • phone in another room — even a switched-off phone on the desk is a distraction;
  • the hardest task first thing — while your attention is fresh;
  • movement — a short walk or a few exercises between blocks restore alertness; WHO recommends 150–⁠300 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults, and physical activity is one of its recommendations for maintaining cognitive function;
  • write things down — to-do lists and notes free up “working memory” for thinking;
  • learn something new — a language, a musical instrument or challenging games train the brain better than passive viewing;
  • social contact — conversations and shared activities support cognitive function with age;
  • hearing and eyesight — check them on time: if you struggle to hear or see, your brain spends more effort just taking things in.

When to get checked

See your GP if:

  • memory or attention problems last several weeks and interfere with work or daily life;
  • people close to you notice that you repeat the same questions, get lost in familiar places or mix up dates;
  • forgetfulness comes with constant tiredness, low mood or weight changes;
  • you started a new medicine and your head has felt “foggy” since.

Your doctor may order a full blood count, ferritin, vitamin B12, thyroid hormones and blood glucose — simple tests that often find the cause. Sudden confusion, weakness on one side of the body or trouble speaking need an ambulance straight away (103 in Ukraine).

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines and do not prevent dementia. Ginkgo and omega‑3 may affect blood clotting: if you take anticoagulants or aspirin, before surgery, and during pregnancy or breastfeeding, take them only with your doctor's approval. Long-lasting memory problems are a reason to see a doctor.

FAQ

Which vitamins are good for the brain in adults?

There are no universal “brain vitamins”. What matters is not being low in vitamin B12, iron or vitamin D, which are checked with blood tests. The rest comes from a varied diet with fish, vegetables, nuts and whole grains.

Does omega-3 help memory?

In the large AREDS2 trial, omega‑3 did not improve memory or thinking in older adults. Oily fish is good in your diet, and a supplement makes sense when you eat little fish.

How can I improve concentration quickly?

The fastest effect comes from simple steps: turn off notifications, put your phone away, drink some water, air the room and take a short walk. In the long run, sleep and regular movement matter most.

Can I take ginkgo and omega-3 together?

Both supplements may affect blood clotting, so discuss combining them with your doctor, especially if you take anticoagulants or aspirin.

Does coffee affect memory?

A moderate amount of coffee in the morning can perk you up, but coffee after lunch disturbs sleep, and sleep is needed to consolidate memory.
