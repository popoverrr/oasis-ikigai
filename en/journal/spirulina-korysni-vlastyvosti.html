Spirulina: nutrients, benefits and how to take it

Published 8 October 2026 · Updated 8 October 2026 · 7 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Spirulina is a blue-green microalga taken as everyday “greens” to complement the diet. Here is what it really contains, what not to expect from it and how to start without discomfort.

What are the benefits of spirulina? It is a blue-green microalga rich in plant protein and pigments (phycocyanin, chlorophyll, beta-carotene), and it also contains iron and other minerals. People take spirulina to complement their diet, especially when they eat few vegetables and greens. Research does not support miraculous effects, and the key thing when choosing is the quality and origin of the raw material.

What spirulina is

Spirulina (Arthrospira platensis, often listed as Spirulina platensis) is a microalga — strictly, a cyanobacterium — that grows in warm alkaline water. Today it is cultivated in controlled ponds.

It differs from chlorella in origin, colour and taste: spirulina is darker, blue-green, with a stronger “sea” note. We compared the two in Chlorella or spirulina: what is the difference.

Nutrients: protein, iron, pigments

  • protein — a large share of the dry weight; but a daily serving of a few grams gives only a few grams of protein, so spirulina does not replace protein foods;
  • pigments — blue phycocyanin, chlorophyll, beta-carotene and other carotenoids;
  • minerals — iron, calcium, magnesium, potassium and zinc;
  • B vitamins, except genuine B12;
  • gamma-linolenic acid, a fatty acid rarely found in plants.

On vitamin B12: spirulina is sometimes promoted as a B12 source for vegans, but according to NIH, much of the B12 in algae is inactive analogues (“pseudovitamin B12”) that humans cannot use. Vegans need another reliable B12 source.

On iron: spirulina contains non-heme iron. If you are iron-deficient, a doctor should decide on supplements based on tests — see Heme iron: how it differs from regular iron.

What research says

Spirulina has been studied for a long time, but most studies are small and short, done in animals or test tubes, with mixed results on cholesterol, blood sugar, immunity or endurance.

The honest conclusion: spirulina is a nutritious supplement that adds protein, pigments and minerals to the diet, but it has no proven “healing” properties. It does not “cleanse the body”, “burn fat” or replace vegetables. WHO recommends a varied diet with at least five portions of fruit and vegetables a day; spirulina can complement that, not replace it.

How to take the tablets

  • Start with a small dose and build up to the recommended serving.
  • Drink enough water.
  • Split the serving into 2–⁠3 doses if there are many tablets.
  • Time of day does not matter — regularity does.

At first you may notice bloating, greenish stools or mild discomfort. If it persists, reduce the serving or take a break.

Spirulina in your diet: how to combine it

Spirulina works best as part of normal eating rather than a separate “cleansing ritual”:

  • in a morning smoothie — powder or crushed tablets are easily masked by banana, berries and spinach, although tablets are simpler with water;
  • with a protein breakfast — eggs, cheese or yoghurt: spirulina complements protein foods rather than replacing them;
  • alongside vegetables — it adds pigments and minerals but does not replace your need for vegetables;
  • apart from coffee and tea — if you are counting on its iron, their polyphenols reduce absorption.

Spirulina is often paired with chlorella to build a daily “green” habit, as is common in Japan; both can be taken together within the servings on the packs. If you also take a multivitamin, check that you are not getting too much iron or beta-carotene. Drink enough water and eat colourful vegetables, fruit, legumes and whole grains — spirulina is a convenient extra, but it does not balance a diet on its own.

Quality and purity: why origin matters

Cyanobacteria from open waters can be contaminated with toxins from other algae (microcystins) and heavy metals. So what matters is controlled cultivation, certified production such as GMP, 100% spirulina without fillers, and a clearly identified maker on the pack. Who should be careful

  • People with phenylketonuria — spirulina contains phenylalanine.
  • People with autoimmune conditions or taking immunosuppressants.
  • People taking anticoagulants — talk to your doctor.
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women and children — only on a doctor's advice.
  • People allergic to algae or seafood.
Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines and do not treat disease. Spirulina does not replace a varied diet and is not a reliable source of vitamin B12.

FAQ

How many spirulina tablets a day?

As many as the maker recommends. For Japan Algae Spirulina 100%, up to 40 tablets a day, starting with 5.

Can children take spirulina?

Supplements for children should be prescribed by a paediatrician. Varied food is the best source of nutrients for a child.

Spirulina in the morning or the evening?

The time of day does not matter. Many people split the serving between morning and lunch.

Does spirulina contain vitamin B12?

Much of the B12 in spirulina is inactive analogues that humans cannot absorb. Vegans need another reliable source of B12.

How is spirulina different from chlorella?

They are different organisms: spirulina is a cyanobacterium and chlorella a green alga. They differ in taste, colour and format.
