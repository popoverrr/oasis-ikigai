Stress and fatigue: daily habits and Japanese supplements

Published 19 October 2026 · Updated 19 October 2026 · 8 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

When stress lasts for weeks, it brings a tiredness that even a day off does not fix. Which daily habits have the best evidence, what is known about magnesium and GABA, and when it is time to ask for help.

For stress and fatigue, simple daily anchors work best: regular sleep, movement, daylight, proper meals and short breaks to recover. Supplements such as magnesium or GABA can be part of a self-care ritual, but they do not replace these habits and do not treat anxiety disorders or depression. If low mood, anxiety or insomnia last more than two weeks, talk to a doctor or therapist.

How stress wears you down

Stress is the body's normal response to a challenge. WHO defines it as a state of worry or mental tension caused by a difficult situation, and in moderate amounts it even helps us focus. The problem starts when the tension does not ease for weeks or months.

During acute stress the body releases adrenaline and cortisol: the heart beats faster, muscles tense and the brain switches to “fight or flight”. When the threat passes, the system returns to calm. With long-term stress that return does not happen, and gradually you may notice:

  • tiredness that sleep and weekends do not fix;
  • light sleep and early waking, or trouble falling asleep;
  • irritability, poor concentration and forgetfulness;
  • tight neck and shoulder muscles, headaches;
  • appetite changes — stress eating or loss of appetite;
  • frequent colds and digestive problems.

In Ukraine, long-term stress has become the experience of millions of people in recent years. That is why it matters not to wait for “better times” but to build small daily anchors that restore your energy now.

Sleep, movement, daylight: the basics

The strongest recovery tools are the simplest ones, recommended by both WHO and the NHS.

Sleep. A consistent wake-up time, even at weekends, matters more than bedtime. Adults need 7–⁠9 hours. For routine, bedroom and evening habits, see Sleep hygiene: 10 rules for better rest.

Movement. Physical activity lowers stress hormones and lifts mood. WHO recommends 150–⁠300 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults — 30 minutes of brisk walking five days a week. No gym needed: walks, stairs instead of the lift, cycling or dancing all count.

Daylight. 15–⁠30 minutes outdoors in the morning helps set your internal clock, making it easier to wake up and feel sleepy on time. This is especially important in winter.

Breaks. Short pauses during the day — a few slow breaths, a few minutes without your phone, a look out of the window — are not wasted time but a way to avoid carrying tension into the evening.

People. Talking to someone close is one of the most reliable ways to reduce stress. The WHO guide “Doing What Matters in Times of Stress” offers simple exercises: noticing your thoughts and feelings, coming back to what matters and taking small steps in line with your values.

Eating under stress

Stress makes us crave sugar, coffee and snacks on the go. A few simple rules help keep energy steady:

  • regular meals — without long gaps that end with a biscuit binge;
  • protein at every meal — eggs, fish, meat, pulses, cottage cheese, yoghurt;
  • vegetables, fruit and whole grains — fibre, B vitamins, magnesium and potassium;
  • nuts and seeds — magnesium and healthy fats;
  • coffee before lunch — caffeine after 2 pm can disturb sleep; more in Energy without extra coffee: 7 daily habits;
  • alcohol is not a way to relax — it worsens sleep and increases anxiety the next day.

If tiredness is severe, it is worth checking basic blood tests: haemoglobin, ferritin, vitamin D, B12 and thyroid hormones. Low iron often hides behind “just being tired” — see Iron deficiency in women: signs, tests and what to do.

Magnesium, GABA and potassium: what is known

“Stress supplements” are a popular category, and honesty matters here.

Magnesium. This mineral is needed for the nervous system and muscles. Low magnesium can show up as fatigue, irritability and muscle cramps. Studies of magnesium for stress and anxiety are small and varied: there are signs of benefit in people with low magnesium intake, but no convincing evidence for everyone. The NIH upper limit for supplemental magnesium in adults is 350 mg a day. On the different forms, see Magnesium: which form to choose and when to take it.

GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid). The brain's main calming neurotransmitter. Whether GABA from a supplement reaches the brain in meaningful amounts is an open question, and the studies are small. More in GABA supplements: what they are and what is known.

Potassium. Needed for nerves and muscles, but not an “anti-stress” mineral in itself. Potassium supplements have serious contraindications, such as kidney disease and blood pressure medicines. Read Potassium: why you need it and when a supplement makes sense before taking it.

Japanese recovery practices

In Japan, self-care is often built into daily rituals rather than saved for a holiday. A few practices that are easy to bring into everyday life:

  • ofuro — an evening hot bath to “close” the day; even 10–⁠15 minutes in warm water helps you unwind before sleep (with heart conditions or high blood pressure, be careful with hot water);
  • shinrin-yoku — “forest bathing”: unhurried walks among trees without your phone or a distance goal; any park will do;
  • hara hachi bu — eating until you are 80% full, slowly and without a screen;
  • ikigai — finding small daily meanings: work you enjoy, people for whom you matter. More in Ikigai: how to find yours and why it matters.

These practices do not magically “remove stress”, but they provide what tense periods lack: a predictable rhythm and moments when you do not have to hurry anywhere.

When to ask for help

Self-help has limits. See your GP, a psychologist or a psychotherapist if:

  • low mood, loss of interest in things you used to enjoy, or anxiety last more than two weeks;
  • insomnia happens several nights a week for a month;
  • you have panic attacks — sudden waves of fear with a racing heart, breathlessness or trembling;
  • you notice you drink more alcohol or take sleeping pills to cope;
  • tiredness gets in the way of work and looking after yourself.

Seek help immediately — call emergency services (103 in Ukraine) or reach out to someone close — if you have thoughts of harming yourself or feel that life is not worth living. This is not weakness; it is a state that professionals know how to help with.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines and do not treat anxiety disorders, depression or insomnia. FAQ

Which vitamins should I take for stress?

There are no universal “stress vitamins”. Start with sleep, movement and food, and if you are very tired, get blood tests (haemoglobin, ferritin, vitamin D, B12, thyroid). Supplements such as magnesium can be discussed with your doctor.

Does magnesium help with anxiety?

The studies are small and mixed: there may be a benefit for people with low magnesium intake, but there is no convincing evidence for everyone. Anxiety that disrupts your life needs a professional.

How quickly can I recover from stress?

There is no quick fix, but the first changes are often noticeable after 1–⁠2 weeks of a steady routine: regular sleep, a daily walk in daylight, proper meals and less caffeine. If you do not feel better, see a doctor.

Can I take GABA with antidepressants?

Seedcoms GABA contains St John's wort, which is dangerous to combine with antidepressants. Not without your doctor's approval.

Can I take sleep supplements and calming remedies together?

Combining several products that act on the nervous system should only be done with a doctor, especially sleeping pills, tranquillisers and alcohol.
