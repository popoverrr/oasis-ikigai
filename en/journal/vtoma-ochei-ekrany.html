Digital eye strain: the 20-20-20 rule and care

Published 19 October 2026 · Updated 19 October 2026 · 8 min read · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Burning, dryness and blurry vision by the end of the working day are familiar to many people who work at a computer. Where digital eye strain comes from and which simple habits really help.

Digital eye strain eases fastest with three habits: regular breaks using the 20-20-20 rule, a well-positioned screen and deliberate blinking. Eye doctors say it does not damage your eyesight permanently, but the symptoms — dryness, burning, blurred vision, headaches — are real and get in the way of work. If they do not go away after rest, have your eyes examined.

What digital eye strain is

Eye specialists call this condition digital eye strain or computer vision syndrome. The American Optometric Association (AOA) describes it as a group of symptoms that appear after long periods using a computer, tablet or phone.

Typical symptoms:

  • dry, burning or gritty eyes;
  • redness and watering;
  • blurred vision, especially when looking from the screen into the distance;
  • headaches and neck or shoulder pain;
  • heavy eyelids by evening.

Why it happens:

  • we blink less — when concentrating on a screen we blink noticeably less often, so the tear film dries out;
  • we focus up close for hours — the muscle that changes the shape of the lens works without a break;
  • contrast and glare — a bright screen in a dark room or a window behind the monitor make the eyes constantly readjust;
  • the wrong screen distance and height — we strain our eyes, neck and shoulders;
  • uncorrected short-sightedness, long-sightedness or astigmatism — even a small mismatch with your glasses adds up over an eight-hour day.

The good news, according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO): digital eye strain does not cause permanent damage. The bad news: without changing your habits, the symptoms come back every day.

The 20-20-20 rule

The simplest and most effective habit: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 metres) away for 20 seconds. This relaxes the focusing muscle, and you blink more at the same time.

How to remember:

  • a timer or reminder on your computer or phone; free apps can dim the screen every 20 minutes;
  • tie it to actions — after every email sent, call or finished task, look out of the window;
  • a “far point” nearby — set up your desk so you can see a window or the far end of the room.

As well as micro-breaks, the AOA suggests a 15-minute break after every two hours of continuous work: stand up, walk around, look into the distance. The rules for children are stricter — see Eyes and screens: protecting a schoolchild's vision.

Lighting, distance and screen settings

Small changes to your workspace remove a good part of the strain.

What to adjustRecommendation
Distance to the monitorAbout arm's length, roughly 50–⁠70 cm
HeightTop of the screen at or slightly below eye level; centre 10–⁠15 cm below your line of sight
BrightnessSimilar to the surrounding light: the screen should not glow in a dark room
Text sizeLarge enough to read without leaning forward; zoom in rather than moving closer
GlareMonitor side-on to the window, not facing it or with the window behind; a matte screen or filter
SmartphoneHold it further from your face than feels natural and increase the font size

Room lighting should be even and soft: overhead light plus a desk lamp that lights your papers but does not shine onto the screen or into your eyes. Working on a laptop in bed or on the sofa in the dark is worse for your eyes and your neck.

If you wear glasses, tell your eye doctor how much time you spend at a computer: sometimes a separate pair set for monitor distance is needed.

Dry eyes and blinking

Dryness is the main complaint of people who work at screens. What helps:

  • deliberate blinking — a few full, slow blinks during every break; with incomplete blinks the lower part of the eye stays exposed and dries out;
  • a screen slightly below eye level — when you look down, your eyes are less open and tears evaporate more slowly;
  • humidifying the air — during the heating season, indoor air is very dry; a humidifier or at least regular airing helps;
  • no airflow in your face — air conditioning and fans should not blow at your desk;
  • lubricating drops (artificial tears) — without vasoconstrictors, which give a quick “white eye” effect but are not suitable for daily use. For Japanese drops and how they differ, see Japanese eye drops: how to choose.

If you wear contact lenses, dryness is felt more strongly. Follow your replacement schedule and give your eyes a rest in glasses from time to time.

Nutrition and supplements

No supplement replaces breaks and a good screen set-up. But a diet that is good for your eyes overall also supports people who spend long hours at screens:

  • oily fish once or twice a week — a source of omega‑3 (DHA, EPA);
  • leafy greens and eggs — lutein and zeaxanthin, the macular pigments; more in Lutein and zeaxanthin: how they work for the eyes;
  • berries and colourful vegetables — anthocyanins and vitamin C;
  • water through the day — dehydration makes dryness feel worse.

If your diet is low in fish and berries, especially in winter, you can discuss supplements with your doctor.

A word on blue-light glasses. A 2023 Cochrane review found no convincing evidence that blue-light filtering lenses reduce eye strain from computer use. The AAO does not recommend them for digital eye strain either. If you find them comfortable, wear them, but breaks and blinking matter more.

When to see an eye doctor

Digital eye strain usually settles with rest. Book an appointment if:

  • symptoms persist even at weekends or after a holiday;
  • your distance or near vision has got worse, or you squint to read;
  • you have double vision, severe eye pain or sensitivity to light;
  • one eye is red and painful or has discharge;
  • you have headaches every day;
  • you have not had an eye test for a long time (adults every 1–⁠2 years, and definitely after 40).

Urgently: a sudden drop in vision, flashes, a “curtain” over your vision or severe pain are not screen fatigue.

Important

This article is for information only and does not replace medical advice. Food supplements are not medicines and do not treat eye disease. Persistent dryness, worsening vision, pain, double vision or flashes are reasons to see an eye doctor. Talk to your doctor before taking supplements if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, or take anticoagulants or other medicines.

FAQ

How many hours can I safely work at a computer?

There is no safe limit in hours for adults; breaks matter more: every 20 minutes, look into the distance for 20 seconds, and every two hours take 15 minutes away from the screen. If your eyes still tire with breaks, have your vision checked.

Do blue-light glasses help?

A 2023 Cochrane review found no convincing evidence that they reduce eye strain. Breaks, the right screen position and deliberate blinking do much more.

Which vitamins help the eyes when working at a computer?

There are no special “screen vitamins”. For eye health in general, omega‑3, lutein and zeaxanthin from fish, greens and eggs matter; if your diet is low in them, discuss supplements with your doctor.

Is night mode harmful?

No, a warmer tone in the evening can feel more comfortable. But it does not replace breaks or relieve dryness.

Can I use eye drops every day?

Lubricating drops without vasoconstrictors can usually be used regularly according to the leaflet. Redness-relief drops are not suitable for daily use.
