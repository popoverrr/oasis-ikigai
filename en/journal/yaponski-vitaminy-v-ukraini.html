Why Japanese supplements are trusted

In Japan, everyday self-care is part of the culture rather than a one-off effort. Supplements are seen as an addition to the diet: small daily servings, with no chasing of “miracle” effects. That is why Japanese makers focus on consistent raw materials, precise dosing and clear “how much per day” instructions.

For a shopper in Ukraine this brings several practical advantages:

  • Transparent packaging. The pack states the daily amount (日目安量 — “approximate amount per day”), the number of servings and the ingredients.
  • Small doses. Japanese formulas are designed for regular use rather than “high-impact” doses.
  • Convenient formats. Tablets, capsules and powders in resealable pouches that are easy to carry.
  • Attention to detail. From the pouch to the fine print on the back, everything is designed so you understand what you are taking.

Japanese standards: FOSHU, FFC and nutrient function claims

Japan has several categories of foods that carry health-related claims. It is worth telling them apart, because they differ in how the claim on the pack has been checked.

FOSHU (特定保健用食品)

Foods for Specified Health Uses. Their health claims undergo a government review, and the pack carries a special mark.

Foods with Function Claims (機能性表示食品)

The maker notifies the Consumer Affairs Agency of Japan before sales begin and submits the scientific basis. The agency does not review it, but publishes the submitted information so that consumers can check it. The maker is responsible for the accuracy of the claims.

Foods with Nutrient Function Claims (栄養機能食品)

Vitamins and minerals with established functions, for which standard wording and content limits apply.

How to avoid fakes

The popularity of Japanese supplements has a downside: products of doubtful origin appear on marketplaces and social media. A few simple rules:

  • buy from shops that clearly state where their products come from and are ready to answer questions about supply;
  • check that the pack is Japanese, with Japanese labelling, an expiry date and the maker's address;
  • be wary of prices far below the market and of sellers without contact details.

How to take and store Japanese supplements

Japanese packaging is usually very precise, so the main rule is simple: do what the back of the pack says. The daily amount is shown next to 日目安量, and the number of tablets or capsules next to 粒. If the pack says “2–⁠4 tablets a day”, start with the lower number and do not exceed the upper limit.

A few practical tips we share with customers:

  • Link it to a habit. It is easiest to remember a supplement if it sits next to something you do every day: breakfast, your toothbrush, morning coffee.
  • Fat-soluble vitamins with a meal. Vitamins D, E, A and omega‑3 are better absorbed with food that contains some fat.
  • Do not double up on the same nutrient. If vitamin D is already in your multi, a separate vitamin D may be unnecessary. Read the ingredients carefully.
  • Store in a cool, dry place. Japanese resealable pouches protect against moisture, but close them tightly and keep them out of the bathroom and direct sunlight.
  • Watch the expiry date. It is printed on the pack, often as year.month.

If you are not sure how to combine several supplements, message our manager on WhatsApp — they will help you read the ingredients and daily amount on your specific pack.
