Eyes and screens: how to protect a schoolchild's vision

Published 30 September 2026 · OASIS IKIGAI editorial team

Online lessons, phones, cartoons — children's eyes work hard at close range. Simple rules from paediatricians help reduce strain, and an eye doctor can spot problems early.

To reduce eye strain in a schoolchild, what matters most is regular breaks from the screen (the 20-20-20 rule), the right distance and lighting, movement and time outdoors, and a yearly eye examination. Eye drops for children — only those whose pack states a children's age, and only after consulting a doctor.

Why children's eyes get tired from screens

When a child looks at a screen for a long time, the eyes keep focusing at one close distance. The American Academy of Pediatrics explains that a break spent looking into the distance lets the eye's lens relax. Without breaks, tiredness, dryness, redness and headaches appear.

Signs to watch for:

  • the child rubs their eyes, blinks often or squints;
  • complains of headaches after lessons or games;
  • leans very close to the notebook or screen;
  • avoids reading or tires of it quickly.

The 20-20-20 rule

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises that every 20 minutes at a screen, a child should look at something 20 feet (about 6 metres) away for 20 seconds. It is easy to make it a habit:

  • set a timer or use a reminder in the learning app;
  • ask the child to look out of the window at a tree or the building opposite;
  • make it a game: who spots something new outside first.

The right screen distance: the 1-2-10 rule

The AAP also suggests the 1-2-10 rule: a smartphone about 1 foot (30 cm) away, a computer or laptop about 2 feet (60 cm) away and a TV about 10 feet (3 m) away. Place the screen at or slightly below eye level: the eyelids strain less and the eyes dry out less.

Light and the workspace

  • Match screen brightness to the room lighting.
  • Avoid glare from windows or lamps on the screen.
  • For homework, use even light without harsh shadows.
  • Chair and desk suited to the child's height: straight back, feet on the floor.
  • No homework on the sofa or in bed — it is bad for both posture and eyes.

Movement, sleep and time outdoors

Eyes benefit not only from breaks but from the whole routine: active play, walks in daylight, enough sleep. A child who sits at a desk all day and at a tablet at home especially needs time outdoors, where the gaze naturally shifts to distant objects.

Nutrition for the eyes

As for the whole body, a varied diet matters for the eyes: vegetables and fruit of different colours, greens, fish, eggs. The WHO recommends at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables a day for children over 10.

Vitamins and supplements for a child — only on a paediatrician's recommendation. The Japanese eye supplements in our catalogue, with lutein and bilberry, are intended for adults; adults can find them in the Vitamins for eyes collection.

Eye drops and children

Japanese eye drops are popular, but most of them are for adults. Many state a minimum age, for example 7+ or 15+.

  • For children — only drops whose label states they are suitable for children of that age.
  • Only after consulting an eye doctor or paediatrician.
  • Drops do not solve the cause of eye strain — screen habits and breaks matter more.

When to take a child to an eye doctor

The WHO notes that uncorrected refractive error (when someone needs glasses but does not have them) is a leading cause of vision impairment in all countries, among both children and adults. And vision problems in schoolchildren are linked to lower educational achievement. That is why regular check-ups matter.

See a doctor if:

  • the child says they cannot see the board well;
  • their eyes hurt, are red for more than two days or have discharge;
  • the child squints, tilts their head or rubs their eyes;
  • double vision or a squint appears.

What to do today

  • Agree the 20-20-20 rule and a timer with your child.
  • Check the screen distance and lighting.
  • Book an eye examination if there has not been one this year.
  • Add a daily walk in daylight.

Important

Not a medicine. This article is for information only and does not replace advice from an eye doctor or paediatrician. Any eye drops or supplements for a child — only on a doctor's recommendation.

FAQ

How much screen time can a child have?

The right amount for your child is best discussed with a paediatrician. What matters most is regular 20-20-20 breaks, the right distance and time outdoors.

What is the 20-20-20 rule?

Every 20 minutes at a screen, look at something 20 feet (about 6 metres) away for 20 seconds. This is advised by the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Can a child use Japanese eye drops?

Only those whose pack states a children's age range, and only after consulting an eye doctor or paediatrician.

How often should a schoolchild's vision be checked?

An eye doctor will recommend a schedule; if there are complaints about vision, pain or redness, do not delay the visit.
